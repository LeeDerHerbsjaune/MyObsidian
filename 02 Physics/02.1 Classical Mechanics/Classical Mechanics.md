> [!abstract] Definition
> **Classical Mechanics** is the branch of physics that studies the motion, equilibrium, and mechanical interactions of physical systems under the influence of forces and constraints.
>
> It provides mathematical frameworks for describing the evolution of mechanical systems in space and time, including particles, systems of particles, rigid bodies, and continuous media.
>
> Classical mechanics is primarily formulated within the framework of Newtonian space and time, where spatial configurations and temporal evolution are treated independently of relativistic effects and quantum phenomena.

## 1. Scope and Objectives

> [!info] Scope
> Classical mechanics investigates the fundamental mechanical properties and behavior of physical systems.
>
> Its principal objectives include:
> 
> - Describing the position, velocity, and acceleration of mechanical systems.
> - Establishing the relationship between forces and motion.
> - Determining the conditions for mechanical equilibrium.
> - Analyzing the transfer and conservation of energy and momentum.
> - Describing rotational and translational motion.
> - Modeling mechanical constraints and interactions.
> - Predicting the temporal evolution and stability of mechanical systems.

## 2. Fundamental Concepts

### 2.1 Mechanical Systems

> [!definition] Mechanical System
> A **mechanical system** is a physical system consisting of one or more interacting bodies or particles whose mechanical behavior is studied through mathematical models.
>
> Common mechanical system models include:
>
> - **Particle:** An idealized body whose spatial dimensions are neglected.
> - **System of Particles:** A collection of interacting particles.
> - **Rigid Body:** An idealized body whose internal distances remain constant.
> - **Continuum:** A material model in which matter is treated as continuously distributed.

### 2.2 Motion and Equilibrium

> [!definition] Motion
> Motion describes the change in the spatial configuration of a physical system with respect to time.
>
> It can involve:
>
> - Translational motion.
> - Rotational motion.
> - General rigid-body motion.
> - Oscillatory motion.

> [!definition] Mechanical Equilibrium
> Mechanical equilibrium is a state in which the mechanical configuration of a system remains unchanged under the applied forces and constraints.
>
> For a particle in an inertial reference frame:
>
> $$\sum_i \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{0}$$
>
> For a rigid body in static equilibrium:
>
> $$\sum_i \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{0}$$
>
> $$\sum_i \boldsymbol{\tau}_i = \mathbf{0}$$

### 2.3 Forces and Interactions

> [!definition] Force
> A **force** is a vector quantity representing a mechanical interaction that can change the momentum of a body.
>
> In Newtonian mechanics:
>
> $$\mathbf{F} = \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}$$
>
> For constant mass:
>
> $$\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$$

### 2.4 Conservation Laws

> [!important] Fundamental Conservation Principles
> Classical mechanical systems can exhibit important conservation laws under appropriate physical conditions:
>
> - **Conservation of Linear Momentum:** Associated with spatial translation symmetry.
> - **Conservation of Angular Momentum:** Associated with rotational symmetry.
> - **Conservation of Energy:** Associated with time-translation symmetry.
>
> These relationships are fundamental to both Newtonian and analytical formulations of mechanics.

## 4. Formulations of Classical Mechanics

> [!abstract] Overview
> Classical mechanics can be formulated through different mathematical approaches. These formulations describe the same underlying physical phenomena under appropriate assumptions, but differ in their mathematical structures and analytical advantages.

### 4.1 Newtonian Mechanics

> [!definition] Newtonian Formulation
> Newtonian mechanics describes motion through Newton's laws, relating forces to changes in momentum.
>
> Its central equation is:
>
> $$\mathbf{F} = \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}$$
>
> This formulation is particularly suitable for force-based analysis of particles and mechanical systems.

### 4.2 Analytical Mechanics

> [!definition] Analytical Formulation
> Analytical mechanics describes mechanical systems using generalized coordinates, variational principles, and energy-based mathematical formulations.
>
> Its principal formulations include:
>
> - **Lagrangian Mechanics:** Describes motion through the Lagrangian and the Euler–Lagrange equations.
> - **Hamiltonian Mechanics:** Describes motion through generalized coordinates, conjugate momenta, and Hamilton's equations.

## 6. Fundamental Principles and Laws

> [!important] Newton's Laws of Motion
> The three fundamental laws governing Newtonian mechanics:
>
> 1. **Law of Inertia:** A body maintains its state of rest or uniform rectilinear motion in the absence of a net external force.
> 2. **Law of Acceleration:** The net external force equals the time derivative of linear momentum.
> 3. **Law of Action and Reaction:** Interacting bodies exert equal and opposite forces on each other.
>
> [!important] Work–Energy Principle
> The net work performed on a particle equals its change in kinetic energy:
>
> $$W_{\mathrm{net}} = \Delta T$$
>
> [!important] Conservation of Linear Momentum
> For an isolated system:
>
> $$\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{dt} = \mathbf{0}$$
>
> [!important] Conservation of Angular Momentum
> When the net external torque is zero:
>
> $$\frac{d\mathbf{L}}{dt} = \mathbf{0}$$
>
> [!important] Conservation of Mechanical Energy
> For a conservative mechanical system:
>
> $$E = T + V = \text{constant}$$
>
> where $T$ is kinetic energy and $V$ is potential energy.

## 7. Knowledge Relationships

### 7.1 Foundation Knowledge

> [!note] Essential Foundations
> - **Classical Physics:** Provides the broader physical framework in which classical mechanics is situated.
> - **Calculus:** Required for describing motion, acceleration, and dynamical evolution.
> - **Linear Algebra:** Required for vector and matrix representations of mechanical quantities.
> - **Differential Equations:** Required for mathematical descriptions of equations of motion.
> - **Vector Calculus:** Supports the mathematical treatment of spatially varying mechanical quantities.

> [!note] Relevant Foundations
> - **Euclidean Geometry:** Provides spatial and geometric relationships.
> - **Multivariable Calculus:** Supports systems described by multiple generalized coordinates.
> - **Calculus of Variations:** Provides the mathematical foundation for variational mechanics.

### 7.2 Downstream Knowledge

> [!note] Relevant Developments
> - **Nonlinear Dynamics:** Extends the study of mechanical systems with nonlinear equations of motion.
> - **Vibrations and Modal Analysis:** Develops the mathematical study of oscillatory mechanical systems.
> - **Celestial Mechanics:** Specializes in gravitational motion of astronomical systems.
> - **Multibody Dynamics:** Studies interconnected rigid and deformable mechanical bodies.
> - **Robotics Dynamics:** Applies classical mechanics to the motion and interaction of robotic mechanisms.

> [!note] Advanced Developments
> - **Hamilton–Jacobi Theory:** Develops an alternative formulation of Hamiltonian mechanics.
> - **Symplectic Geometry:** Provides the geometric structure underlying Hamiltonian mechanics.
> - **Classical Field Theory:** Extends classical mechanical principles to continuous fields.
> - **Statistical Mechanics:** Connects microscopic mechanical descriptions with macroscopic thermodynamic behavior.

## 8. Assumptions and Limitations

> [!warning] Classical Approximation
> Classical mechanics is based on assumptions that limit its applicability:
>
> - **Non-relativistic Motion:** Usually assumes velocities much smaller than the speed of light.
> - **Classical Space and Time:** Uses absolute time and Galilean spacetime.
> - **Deterministic Evolution:** Given appropriate initial conditions and a well-defined dynamical model, the evolution is determined by its equations of motion.
> - **Classical Description:** Does not account for quantum phenomena.
> - **Idealized Models:** Often uses ideal particles, rigid bodies, frictionless constraints, or continuous media.
>
> Relativistic mechanics becomes necessary when relativistic effects are significant, while quantum mechanics is required when quantum effects cannot be neglected.
